1. Hypothesis Summary:
The hypothesis suggests that in hypoxic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) mediates metabolic reprogramming that leads to increased lactate production. This lactate, upon acting on its receptors, induces tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) to adopt an M2 (pro-tumoral) phenotype. These M2 macrophages then secrete cytokines which, through paracrine signaling, upregulate the serine synthesis pathway in cancer cells via induction of PHGDH. The increased serine metabolism boosts NADPH production and improves redox balance, which in turn promotes resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The hypothesis also states that advanced tools—such as single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial metabolomics, and CRISPR screens—will be utilized to delineate the underlying molecular loop, with the goal of developing targeted interventions that block either lactate-mediated macrophage activation or serine metabolism to overcome therapeutic resistance.

2. Originality Assessment:
The hypothesis integrates several known biological processes in HCC—namely, HIF-induced metabolic reprogramming, lactate production, immune modulation of TAMs, serine metabolism, and treatment resistance. While each of these individual components has been studied previously, the integrated feedback loop linking lactate-induced macrophage polarization to paracrine upregulation of the serine synthesis pathway represents a novel perspective. Specifically, the proposed link between macrophage-derived cytokines promoting metabolic adaptation in cancer cells to facilitate redox homeostasis and therapy resistance is innovative. The use of combined advanced technologies also strengthens the novelty by proposing a multifaceted investigative approach that could challenge established paradigms in HCC therapy resistance.

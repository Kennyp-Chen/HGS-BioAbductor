1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis proposes that in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), oxidative stress triggers a specific upregulation of m6A writer enzymes (METTL3 and METTL14). This upregulation leads to increased m6A modification on a subset of hypoxia-responsive long noncoding RNAs (for example, a candidate termed lncRNA-X). The hypothesis further posits that this m6A “tagging” enhances the binding affinity of these lncRNAs to RNA-binding proteins (specifically IGF2BP2), which in turn stabilizes the mRNA of HIF-1α. The accumulation of HIF-1α protein then drives angiogenesis, further reinforces oxidative stress, and thus establishes a positive feedback loop that accelerates tumor progression. The refined model is intended to be tested with modern techniques such as m6A-seq (to map modifications), CLIP-seq (to detect lncRNA-RBP interactions), and CRISPR-based epitranscriptomic interventions (to modulate specific steps) in both in vitro and in vivo settings.

2. Originality Assessment

The hypothesis presents a novel integrative view by linking:
 • Oxidative stress with the regulation of epitranscriptomic modifiers (m6A writers),
 • m6A modifications on hypoxia-responsive lncRNAs, and
 • Post-transcriptional stabilization of a critical oncogenic factor (HIF-1α) via RNA-binding proteins (IGF2BP2).

Within the omics and cancer research fields, while m6A modifications in cancer and the roles of individual components (such as METTL3/METTL14, IGF2BP2, and HIF-1α) have been independently studied, the specific mechanism that ties these events together in a self-amplifying loop under oxidative stress conditions in HCC is innovative. This interdisciplinary link between epitranscriptomics, noncoding RNA function, and tumor microenvironment dynamics (hypoxia/oxidative stress) distinguishes the hypothesis from more traditional, single-component studies.

 • The hypothesis presents a coherent sequence: oxidative stress → upregulation of m6A writers → modification of specific lncRNAs → altered binding with IGF2BP2 → stabilization of HIF-1α mRNA → increased HIF-1α protein levels → angiogenesis and enhanced oxidative stress → feedback loop. This stepwise progression is logical and testable.
 • The proposed experimental techniques (m6A-seq, CLIP-seq, CRISPR-based interventions) are well aligned with the mechanistic questions raised.

Strengths:

 • Testability: Detailed experimental strategies increase the possibility to systematically validate each step of the proposed mechanism.

Potential Weaknesses and Points Needing Further Evidence:
 • Causality versus correlation: While oxidative stress may be associated with increased METTL3/METTL14 expression, the precise signaling pathway by which oxidative stress selectively enhances these writers needs further clarification.
 • Specificity: The hypothesis focuses on “a subset” of hypoxia-responsive lncRNAs (e.g., lncRNA-X), but does not delineate the criteria or mechanism behind their selective m6A modification. It would be important to determine whether other lncRNAs are similarly modified or if there is specificity in the METTL3/METTL14 targeting mechanism.
 • Binding Affinity Modulation: The mechanism by which m6A modification alters the binding affinity for IGF2BP2 on lncRNA-X is assumed but requires robust structural and interaction data for validation.
 • Feedback Complexity: The proposed self-amplifying loop, where increased HIF-1α both drives angiogenesis and reinforces oxidative stress, is plausible but could involve additional regulatory nodes. This may complicate the interpretation of experimental data.

Conclusion:
The hypothesis is conceptually strong and innovative, nicely bridging oxidative stress with epitranscriptomic regulation, noncoding RNA biology, and oncogenic signaling in HCC. However, there are weaknesses primarily in the detailed mechanistic explanations and specificity of the interactions proposed.

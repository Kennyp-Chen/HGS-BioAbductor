1. Hypothesis Summary  
─────────────────────────────  
The hypothesis posits that lactate produced by tumor cells induces histone lactylation in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This post‐translational modification thereby epigenetically reprograms MDSCs into a more immunosuppressive phenotype. The downstream implication is that targeting either lactate production (for example, via interference with glycolytic enzymes such as lactate dehydrogenase) or blocking the lactate-induced histone modifications could weaken MDSC-driven immunosuppression and, as a result, improve the clinical effectiveness of cancer immunotherapies.

2. Originality Assessment  
─────────────────────────────  
• The idea that tumor-derived lactate can act as a signaling molecule to modify the tumor microenvironment is well recognized, including its role in modulating immune responses.  
• The concept of histone lactylation as an epigenetic marker is a relatively recent discovery, and its functional implications in immune cells are still being elucidated.  
• While there have been studies exploring lactate’s role in the tumor microenvironment and histone modifications generally, linking tumor‐derived lactate specifically to histone lactylation in MDSCs—and consequentially to an enhancement of their immunosuppressive functions—is innovative. It extends the concept of metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk to a new effector cell type (MDSCs) within the context of cancer immunotherapy.  
• Therefore, this hypothesis offers a fresh perspective by connecting metabolic byproducts to immune regulation via epigenetic modifications, potentially opening up novel therapeutic avenues.

• Logical Coherence:  
  ◦ The hypothesis builds on two established observations: (a) tumor cells produce lactate as a byproduct of aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and (b) lactate can serve as a precursor for histone lactylation, an emerging epigenetic modification.  
  ◦ It logically extends these observations by suggesting that lactate’s effect on histone modifications in MDSCs leads to enhanced immunosuppression—a key barrier to effective cancer immunotherapy.  
• The logical chain is attractive because it integrates metabolic and epigenetic regulation; however, the following aspects require careful consideration:

  ◦ Causality versus Correlation: Although lactate levels and immunosuppressive phenotypes may correlate, demonstrating direct causality (i.e., that histone lactylation is both necessary and sufficient for the observed MDSC reprogramming) is essential.
  ◦ Mechanistic Underpinnings: While histone lactylation is a newly recognized modification, existing evidence on its downstream gene expression changes, particularly in MDSCs, is limited. The hypothesis would benefit from a detailed mechanistic framework.
• Evidence and Theoretical Grounding:  
  ◦ Current literature provides some basis, but empirical data specifically linking tumor lactate, histone lactylation in MDSCs, and their immunosuppressive functions is in its infancy.
  ◦ The efficacy aspect (i.e., that interfering with this pathway will enhance immunotherapeutic responses) aligns with the wider goals of improving cancer treatments but requires substantial experimental backing.

Conclusion:  
The hypothesis is conceptually strong and innovative in correlating tumor metabolism with epigenetic modulation of immune cells. Nonetheless, its novelty also underscores several gaps that must be addressed to validate the proposed mechanism and its therapeutic implications.

• Lack of direct causal evidence linking lactate-induced histone lactylation in MDSCs to their enhanced immunosuppressive activity.  
• Unclear whether lactate acts solely on MDSCs or if it also influences other cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby complicating targeted therapeutic approaches.  
• Insufficient mechanistic details on how histone lactylation changes the transcriptional program of MDSCs to lead to a more suppressive phenotype.  
• Potential redundancies and compensatory pathways in both metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation that might mitigate the impact of targeting lactate production or histone lactylation.

1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis suggests that lactate secreted by tumor cells induces histone lactylation within myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This epigenetic modification is proposed to reprogram MDSCs into a state with enhanced immunosuppressive activity, thereby contributing to tumor immune evasion. Consequently, intervening either by reducing lactate production or by blocking lactate‐mediated histone modifications is posited as a therapeutic strategy to diminish MDSC function and bolster the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies.

──────────────────────────────
2. Originality Assessment

The hypothesis integrates two emerging areas of research: the role of metabolic byproducts (in this case, lactate) in the tumor microenvironment and the relatively novel concept of histone lactylation as an epigenetic regulatory mechanism. While the concept of lactate influencing immune cell behavior is not entirely new, its specific link to histone lactylation in MDSCs and the subsequent reprogramming to an enhanced immunosuppressive state is innovative. This multi-faceted angle distinguishes the hypothesis from established paradigms that primarily focus on metabolic alterations or standard immune checkpoints, offering a fresh perspective on the interplay between metabolism, epigenetics, and immune regulation in cancer.

──────────────────────────────
3. Validity and Strength Evaluation

Logical Coherence:
• The hypothesis is internally coherent. It builds on known principles: tumor cells often produce high levels of lactate due to aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), and recent studies have identified histone lactylation as a mechanism for epigenetic regulation.
• It logically extends these ideas to MDSCs, known contributors to immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment, creating a plausible link between metabolic byproducts and immune cell reprogramming.

Evidence and Theoretical Grounding:
• There is emerging evidence that lactate can influence immune cells and that histone modifications can alter gene expression profiles. The concept of histone lactylation adds a novel layer to epigenetic regulation, though its functional implications in different cell types are still under investigation.
• While evidence exists for lactate-induced changes in other cell types, direct evidence linking tumor-derived lactate to histone lactylation specifically in MDSCs is still sparse. This is a potential gap, as the hypothesis extrapolates findings from other cell contexts without confronting the unique biology of MDSCs.
• The assumption that such epigenetic reprogramming directly translates to a more immunosuppressive phenotype is plausible but may require further mechanistic and contextual evidence, especially considering the heterogeneity of MDSC populations (e.g., monocytic versus granulocytic).

Strength of the Relationship:
• The link between tumor metabolism and immune evasion is well-established, lending credence to the idea that metabolic intermediates can act as signaling molecules. However, the precise molecular cascade—from extracellular lactate to intracellular histone lactylation, and then to transcriptional reprogramming—needs clearer articulation and supporting empirical data.
• The therapeutic implication (targeting lactate production or histone lactylation) is ambitious and attractive, but there may be challenges. For example, lactate has multiple functions both inside and outside the cell, and broad inhibition might have unintended consequences on normal physiology.

4. Conclusion and Expert Recommendations

Notable Weaknesses and Gaps:
• Direct experimental evidence linking tumor-derived lactate with histone lactylation specifically in MDSCs is currently limited. The hypothesis would benefit from direct demonstration in relevant cell models and in vivo systems.
• The heterogeneity of MDSCs is not addressed. Different subsets might respond differently to lactate and exhibit distinct epigenetic landscapes.
• Alternative mechanisms of MDSC reprogramming are not considered. It is important to evaluate whether other metabolic byproducts or signaling pathways might also contribute to the enhanced immunosuppressive state.
• Potential off-target effects of strategies aimed at inhibiting lactate production need to be carefully considered since lactate is also involved in normal metabolic processes in non-tumor cells.

Actionable Suggestions for Enhancing Scientific Rigor:
• Design and conduct studies that directly measure histone lactylation levels in MDSCs isolated from tumor-bearing models versus controls. Use techniques such as chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) to map lactylation marks and correlate them with gene expression changes.
• Investigate the functional effects of modulating lactate levels on MDSC activity using in vitro assays (e.g., T-cell suppression assays) and in vivo tumor models.
• Consider stratifying MDSCs into different subsets and assessing whether lactate-induced lactylation occurs uniformly or preferentially in a particular population.
• Explore combinatory strategies that target both lactate production and other epigenetic modifications to assess synergistic impacts on tumor immune evasion.
• Include validation studies using pharmacological inhibitors or genetic knockdown approaches to block lactate production and/or lactylation enzymes, while monitoring potential side effects on normal tissues.

Overall, while the hypothesis offers a creative and potentially impactful link between tumor metabolism, epigenetic regulation, and immune suppression, strengthening the evidentiary support and addressing the outlined gaps will be crucial in making it more compelling and translatable to therapeutic strategies.

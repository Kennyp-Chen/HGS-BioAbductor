1. Hypothesis Summary:
The hypothesis posits that lactate produced by tumors—a result of their glycolytic metabolism—is absorbed by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and induces histone lactylation. This epigenetic change allegedly shifts MDSCs into a highly immunosuppressive phenotype. The mechanism is proposed to work in tandem with established pro-tumorigenic signals such as the GM‑CSF/STAT3 pathway, resulting in upregulation of immunosuppressive genes including ARG1 and iNOS. The resultant state inhibits T cell activity and promotes tumor immune evasion. Finally, the hypothesis suggests that therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target lactate production/transport/lactylation and the GM‑CSF/STAT3 pathway could disrupt MDSC function, slow tumor progression, and improve the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies. Validation of this proposed causal chain is to be performed using modern techniques like single-cell RNA sequencing and high-resolution epigenomic profiling.

2. Originality Assessment:
This hypothesis offers an innovative perspective by linking tumor metabolism (lactate production) directly to epigenetic reprogramming (histone lactylation) in immune cells. Although the role of lactate in the tumor microenvironment and the function of MDSCs in immune suppression are well-known, the idea that lactate drives histone modifications in MDSCs to potentiate their immunosuppressive function introduces a novel mechanism. Additionally, the concept of combined therapeutic interventions targeting both metabolic/epigenetic processes and established GM‑CSF/STAT3 signaling pathways is a forward-thinking integrative approach that distinguishes this hypothesis from more traditional single-target strategies.

3. Validity and Strength Evaluation:
The hypothesis is logically structured with clearly defined steps:
 • It starts from the biological observation that tumor cells produce large amounts of lactate.
 • It connects lactate to epigenetic modification (histone lactylation) in MDSCs.
 • It proposes that these epigenetic changes cause upregulation of genes (e.g., ARG1 and iNOS) involved in immunosuppression.
 • It suggests that lactate-induced changes work alongside known pro-tumor signals (GM‑CSF/STAT3), culminating in enhanced immune evasion.
 • Finally, it lays out combination therapy strategies and the modern techniques needed for experimental validation.

While each step is conceptually coherent, the key challenge lies in the degree of causality – i.e., how directly lactate-induced histone lactylation drives the immunosuppressive phenotype compared to contributing factors such as other cytokines or tumor-derived signals. The hypothesis would benefit from further elaboration on the mechanistic pathways linking lactate uptake, specific histone modifications, and gene regulation. It assumes that blocking lactate and inhibiting GM‑CSF/STAT3 will have a synergistic effect, but the potential for compensatory mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment needs careful consideration.

Identifying the receptors, transporters, or mediators that facilitate lactate’s entry and subsequent engagement of lactylation enzymes would fortify the causal link.

1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis posits that tumor-derived lactate—generated as a byproduct of the Warburg effect (enhanced glycolysis in tumor cells)—is taken up by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), where it induces histone lactylation. This epigenetic modification is proposed to reprogram MDSCs towards a more potent immunosuppressive phenotype. In this reprogramming, lactate-driven histone modifications work alongside known pro-tumorigenic pathways (notably the GM‑CSF/STAT3 axis) to upregulate immunosuppressive genes such as ARG1 and iNOS. The combined activity of these pathways is thought to inhibit T cell functions and contribute to tumor immune evasion. The hypothesis further suggests that a therapeutic strategy targeting both lactate production/transport (or its downstream lactylation effects) and the GM‑CSF/STAT3 pathway could reduce MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, slow tumor progression, and improve cancer immunotherapy outcomes. Validation of this causal chain is proposed via advanced methods like single-cell RNA sequencing and high-resolution epigenomic profiling.

2. Originality Assessment

The proposed hypothesis is innovative in several respects:

• While histone lactylation has been described (notably in macrophages and in various contexts of cellular reprogramming), applying this concept specifically to MDSCs and linking it with the GM‑CSF/STAT3 pathway in cancer immunosuppression represents a novel integration of metabolic and epigenetic regulation.
• The therapeutic approach, which combines blockade of both lactate-mediated epigenetic changes (through inhibition of lactate production/transport or lactylation enzyme activity) and the classical pro-immunosuppressive GM‑CSF/STAT3 signaling, provides a multifaceted intervention that distinguishes it from many existing strategies that target a single pathway.

Overall, while aspects of tumor lactate metabolism and GM‑CSF/STAT3 signaling are well established, the integration through histone lactylation in MDSCs and the dual targeting strategy are original and provide new perspectives in the field of cancer immunotherapy.

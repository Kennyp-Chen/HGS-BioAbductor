1. Hypothesis Summary  
─────────────────────────────  
The hypothesis proposes that by targeting the specific post‐translational modifications (PTMs) which favor the formation of the PKM2 dimer and its subsequent nuclear translocation, it is possible to shift PKM2 equilibrium toward its tetrameric, enzymatically active form. In its tetrameric conformation, PKM2 primarily functions in glycolysis rather than acting as a transcriptional coactivator. The switch away from the dimeric form is hypothesized to disrupt the transcriptional activation of glycolytic genes. Ultimately, this metabolic and transcriptional modulation is expected to reverse resistance to sorafenib, a standard therapeutic agent used in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

─────────────────────────────  
2. Originality Assessment  
─────────────────────────────  
• The role of PKM2 in cancer metabolism and its regulation through conformational changes is a well‐recognized area of study.  
• However, focusing specifically on targeting the PTMs that favor PKM2’s dimer formation and nuclear localization to indirectly influence drug resistance in HCC introduces a novel therapeutic angle that links metabolic regulation to resistance mechanisms.  
• While the modulation of PKM2 activity has been explored previously, the direct connection to overcoming sorafenib resistance by stabilizing the tetrameric form appears to distinguish this hypothesis, thereby offering potential innovative insights in the context of HCC treatment.  
• Thus, the hypothesis offers an innovative perspective by connecting post‐translational regulation of PKM2, glycolytic gene transcription, and chemotherapeutic resistance in a single mechanistic framework.

─────────────────────────────  
3. Validity and Strength Evaluation  
─────────────────────────────  
• Logical Coherence:  
 – The hypothesis is logically structured. It builds on established knowledge that PKM2 exists in different conformations with distinct cellular roles. The tetrameric form primarily catalyzes glycolysis, whereas the dimeric form can translocate to the nucleus to support gene transcription.  
 – The argument follows that shifting the balance to the tetrameric state should reduce nuclear PKM2 activity, thereby diminishing the transcriptional activation of genes associated with enhanced glycolysis—a metabolic feature of drug-resistant cancer cells.  
• Evidence and Theoretical Grounding:  
 – There is substantial literature on the dual roles of PKM2 in metabolism and gene regulation. The hypothesis leverages existing evidence linking PKM2 conformational dynamics with its function.  
 – However, the hypothesis assumes that PTM-targeted interventions can sufficiently and selectively stabilize the tetrameric form without eliciting off-target effects or compromising other cellular functions.  
 – In addition, while the conceptual connection to sorafenib resistance in HCC is appealing, the direct causative link between PKM2 nuclear activity and drug resistance would benefit from more in-depth mechanistic studies or preliminary data to reinforce this argument.  
• Strengths:  
 – Integrates metabolic reprogramming with drug resistance—a high-priority area in cancer research.  
 – Targets a modifiable point (PTMs) which could be accessible to therapeutic intervention.  
• Weaknesses:  
 – Lack of detail on which specific PTMs to target may leave the hypothesis too broad. Identifying the key modifications (e.g., phosphorylation, acetylation, oxidation) is essential for experimental design.  
 – The hypothesis presumes that altering PKM2 conformation will directly impact sorafenib sensitivity. However, sorafenib resistance is a multifactorial phenomenon that may also involve other signaling pathways and cellular processes not addressed by simply modulating PKM2 conformation.  
 – It is also necessary to consider whether stabilizing the tetrameric form might inadvertently affect normal cells’ metabolism, thereby raising potential safety or specificity concerns.

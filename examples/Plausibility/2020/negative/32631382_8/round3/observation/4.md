1. Observation extraction:
 • Abstract 1 shows that inhibiting miR‐1914‑5p in HepG2 hepatocarcinoma cells markedly rewires metabolism – increasing energy expenditure, reducing cell doubling time, and decreasing migration/invasion.
 • Abstract 2 reports that a treatment combining nutrient restriction (i.e., fasting) with sorafenib and metformin (NR‐S:M) suppresses both glycolytic and mitochondrial respiratory functions, inducing energetic collapse, cell cycle arrest (increased SubG1 phase), and apoptosis.
 • Abstract 3 demonstrates that miR‐199a‑5p is a suppressor of glycolysis in liver cancer. It directly targets hexokinase‑2 (HK2), reducing glycolysis, lactate production, and tumorigenic attributes. Low miR‑199a‑5p levels in tumors correlate with worse patient outcomes.

2. Causal analysis (individual):
 Observation 1:
  – Cause already established? Yes. The functional inhibition of miR‑1914‑5p is shown to reprogram metabolism.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Intermittent fasting restoring/boosting a broader miRNA network (including effects on miR‑199a‑5p and miR‑202) is not directly linked to the miR‑1914‑5p effects. The known cause in this study is the direct inhibition of this specific miRNA.
  – Explanation: This observation is explained by mechanisms already established (miR‑1914‑5p’s role), so it is not a missing piece.
 
 Observation 2:
  – Cause already established? Yes. The anti-proliferative and energy‐collapse effects of NR‑S:M treatment have been characterized via nutrient restriction and mitochondrial/glycolytic inhibition.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: While intermittent fasting is part of the hypothesis, the observed outcome in Abstract 2 is an overall energy collapse (with both glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration suppressed). In contrast, the hypothesis predicts that intermittent fasting shifts the metabolism from high‐rate aerobic glycolysis toward enhanced oxidative phosphorylation.
  – Explanation: The established mechanism (nutrient restriction–induced energetic collapse) already explains the observation; the proposed miRNA network providing a shift to oxidative phosphorylation is not clearly reflected and is therefore not a novel explanatory piece.
 
 Observation 3:

  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: The hypothesis claims that intermittent fasting prevents the downregulation of miR‑199a‑5p and also stimulates miR‑202, together suppressing key glycolytic enzymes (HK2 and PKM2). However, since miR‑199a‑5p’s tumor‐suppressive effects are already known and documented, this component is not a novel mechanism.
  – Explanation: The observation in Abstract 3 is already explained in the literature, so the hypothesis does not significantly add a missing piece here.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: Intermittent fasting is known to affect tumor metabolism and could conceivably modulate expression of certain miRNAs (like miR‑199a‑5p). However, the observations from Abstracts 2 and 3 already have established explanations—fasting as a nutrient-deprivation strategy generally leads to energetic collapse, and the role of miR‑199a‑5p in suppressing glycolysis via HK2 is well characterized. The hypothesis’s proposed network (including a novel role for miR‑202) and the shift toward oxidative phosphorylation do not clearly align with the reported experimental outcomes (e.g., overall mitochondrial impairment in Abstract 2). Overall, while intermittent fasting as an intervention does produce effects on cancer energy metabolism, the detailed mechanistic proposal (with miRNA network modulation causing a glycolysis-to-OXPHOS shift and immune reactivation) does not emerge as a missing explanatory piece based on these observations.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: None of the data outright contradict the idea that fasting affects metabolism. However, the observation from Abstract 2 shows that under nutrient restriction (in combination with sorafenib and metformin), both glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration are depressed, which conflicts with the hypothesis’s prediction of a metabolic shift favoring oxidative phosphorylation. In addition, the specific involvement of miR‑202 is not supported by any of the abstracts. These inconsistencies suggest that while fasting does impact metabolism, the proposed novel causal chain involving a shift to oxidative phosphorylation and enhanced anti-tumor immunity via miRNA network modulation may not be the best explanation of the observed phenomena.

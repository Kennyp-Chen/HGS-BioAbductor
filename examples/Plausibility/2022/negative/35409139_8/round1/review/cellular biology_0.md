1. Hypothesis Summary:
The hypothesis proposes that in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), inhibiting methionine adenosyltransferase (MAT)—thereby reducing the production of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and methylthioadenosine (MTA)—can alleviate T cell exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment. This alleviation is expected to restore T cell cytotoxicity, which in turn would enhance the effectiveness of checkpoint inhibitor-based immunotherapies.

2. Originality Assessment:
The hypothesis is innovative in that it links metabolic intervention with immune modulation in a cancer context. Targeting MAT to modulate SAM and MTA levels and thereby influence T cell exhaustion represents a novel approach when compared to traditional methods focusing solely on immune checkpoint blockade. While the concept of altering tumor metabolism to improve immune responses has been explored in various contexts, specifically targeting MAT in HCC to affect T cell function provides a unique perspective that differentiates it from existing studies.

3. Validity and Strength Evaluation:
The hypothesis is logically structured and connects metabolic pathways to immune cell functionality, which is a promising avenue in cancer immunotherapy research. The rationale is as follows:

 • T cell exhaustion is a major barrier to effective immunotherapy, and alleviating it could improve cytotoxic T cell responses against tumors.
 • Combining such a metabolic intervention with checkpoint inhibitors could synergistically enhance anti-tumor effects.

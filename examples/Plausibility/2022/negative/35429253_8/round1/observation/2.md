1. Observation extraction:
 • Abstract 1 shows that the gut microbiota transforms bile acids into metabolites (e.g., deoxycholic acid) and produces SCFAs, with some metabolites contributing to gastrointestinal carcinogenesis via inflammation, oxidative DNA damage, and cell cycle modulation.  
 • Abstract 2 explains that bile acids, at supraphysiological versus reference concentrations, have dual (pro‐ and anticarcinogenic) effects mediated by receptors such as FXR and TGR5, with differences in receptor expression determining cancer cell responses.  
 • Abstract 3 details that dysbiosis (altered gut microbiota) in metabolic diseases (like NAFLD) is linked to increased secondary bile acids, changes in FXR expression, and immune/inflammatory responses that promote liver carcinogenesis.

2. Causal analysis (individual):
 Observation 1 – Gut microbiota metabolizes primary bile acids to toxic secondary bile acids known to be involved in colon carcinogenesis.
  a. Cause already established? Yes – microbial transformation of bile acids into metabolites (e.g., deoxycholic acid) is well documented.
  b. Could the hypothesis be a causal factor? Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Yes, if a high‐fat diet alters the gut microbiota, it may increase the production of hydrophobic secondary bile acids.  
  c. Novel explanation? The mechanism of bile acid transformation is established; attributing it solely to a high‐fat diet–driven change is consistent with known processes, so this is “not a missing piece.”

 Observation 2 – Bile acids acting via receptors (FXR and TGR5) exert both tumor‐promoting and tumor‐suppressing effects depending on their concentrations.
  a. Cause already established? Yes – receptor‐mediated effects by bile acids are recognized.
  b. Could the hypothesis be a causal factor? Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Yes, altered levels of hydrophobic secondary bile acids could engage FXR and TGR5 signaling.
  c. Novel explanation? The involvement of TGR5 and FXR is known; however, linking them specifically to epigenetic reprogramming that induces a cancer stem cell phenotype in colon cells is not clearly documented in the abstracts. Thus, while the signal transduction is known, the proposed downstream effect (cancer stem cell phenotype via epigenetic reprogramming) is not a clearly established part of the observed mechanisms – “not a missing piece” compared to more established inflammatory or DNA damage routes.

 Observation 3 – Dysbiosis and subsequent microbiome shifts (as observed in NAFLD and cirrhosis) correlate with increased secondary bile acids and altered receptor expression which contribute to carcinogenesis.
  a. Cause already established? Partially – the link between dysbiosis, increased secondary bile acids, and cancer via inflammatory and metabolic pathways is well recognized.
  b. Could the hypothesis be a causal factor? Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Yes, if a high‐fat diet induces dysbiosis, it may produce hydrophobic secondary bile acids that impact receptor signaling.
  c. Novel explanation? While the connection between dysbiosis and cancer is established, the idea that epigenetic reprogramming via TGR5/FXR specifically induces a cancer stem cell phenotype introduces a new twist. Nonetheless, the abstracts generally favor explanations via inflammation, innate immune activation, and oxidative stress. Therefore, this detail is intriguing but doesn’t appear as the missing cornerstone mechanism in the current observations – “not a missing piece.”

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: Yes. A high‐fat diet–induced alteration in the gut microbiota could shift bile acid metabolism toward more hydrophobic secondary bile acids that interact with FXR and TGR5. That being said, the dominant narrative provided in the abstracts highlights mechanisms via inflammation, oxidative DNA damage, and altered receptor expression rather than a clear epigenetic reprogramming toward a cancer stem cell phenotype. In other words, while the hypothesis is consistent with parts of the observed data, its emphasis on epigenetic reprogramming driving a cancer stem cell phenotype is not the primary or novel mechanism suggested by these abstracts.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: None of the observations explicitly contradict the hypothesis. All abstracts acknowledge the role of bile acid metabolism and receptor-mediated effects in cancer. However, the established mechanisms (e.g., inflammation, oxidative stress) present more conventional explanations, and the specific step of “epigenetic reprogramming to induce a cancer stem cell phenotype” is neither confirmed nor strongly hinted at by the observations.

5. Conclusion:
 The hypothesis aligns with observations showing that gut microbiota influences bile acid metabolism and that receptors like FXR/TGR5 mediate cancer-related processes. However, the specific causal route—high-fat diet–induced microbiota alterations leading to hydrophobic secondary bile acids that, via epigenetic reprogramming, induce a cancer stem cell phenotype—is not directly supported by the major observations. Alternative, well-supported explanations such as inflammation, oxidative DNA damage, and conventional receptor-mediated effects are more established.

1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis states that a high‐fat diet alters the gut microbiota, leading to an increased production of hydrophobic secondary bile acids. These bile acids are proposed to bind and activate the receptors TGR5 and FXR, which in turn trigger epigenetic reprogramming in colonic epithelial cells. This epigenetic change is suggested to promote the emergence of a cancer stem cell phenotype, thereby initiating and advancing colorectal cancer.

──────────────────────────────
2. Originality Assessment

• Novel Integration: While studies have independently linked high‐fat diets, gut dysbiosis, and bile acids with colorectal cancer, this hypothesis innovatively integrates these components by focusing on receptor-mediated (TGR5 and FXR) epigenetic reprogramming as a bridge toward acquiring a cancer stem cell phenotype.

• Innovative Perspective: The notion that bile acid receptor activation could induce cancer stem cell traits through epigenetic modifications is a relatively novel angle. It steps beyond merely associating dietary fat with cancer risk, proposing a detailed molecular pathway that connects environmental (dietary) factors with intracellular epigenetic changes.

• Context in Existing Research: Although previous work has explored the roles of bile acids and their receptors in cancer, linking them directly with epigenetic reprogramming to promote cancer stem cell features is an emerging concept. This marks the hypothesis as a potentially valuable contribution to the field of omics and cancer biology, pending further empirical support.

──────────────────────────────
3. Validity and Strength Evaluation

• Logical Coherence: 
   - There is a logical sequence: High-fat diet → altered microbiota → increased hydrophobic secondary bile acids → activation of bile acid receptors (TGR5 and FXR) → epigenetic changes → cancer stem cell induction → oncogenesis.
   - Each step builds upon known biological processes. High-fat diets are known to influence microbiota composition, and secondary bile acids have been implicated in carcinogenesis.

• Evidence and Gaps:
   - There is substantial literature supporting parts of the chain, such as the impact of high-fat diets on the microbiome and the carcinogenic potential of bile acids.
   - However, the explicit link between TGR5/FXR activation and specific epigenetic modifications that induce a cancer stem cell phenotype is less well charted. The hypothesis assumes that receptor activation leads directly to a reprogramming that confers stem-like properties, but the exact epigenetic mechanisms (e.g., histone modification, DNA methylation, non-coding RNA modulation) remain unspecified.
   - It is also important to consider whether the activation of TGR5 and FXR has consistent, pro-tumorigenic outcomes in the context of colorectal tissues, as receptor signaling can be context-dependent.

• Theoretical Grounding:
   - The hypothesis is grounded in current knowledge about the roles of gut microbiota, bile acids, and receptor signaling in cancer.
   - However, it would benefit from a more detailed mechanistic model that explicitly connects receptor activation to established epigenetic changes known to promote stemness.

4. Conclusion and Expert Recommendations

Conclusion:
• The hypothesis presents an innovative and plausible mechanism linking dietary habits to colorectal cancer via epigenetic reprogramming. It effectively integrates multiple disciplines—nutrition, microbiology, molecular signaling, and epigenetics—into a cohesive model of tumorigenesis.
• Notable weaknesses include a lack of detailed mechanistic insights on how TGR5 and FXR activation leads to specific epigenetic alterations that induce a cancer stem cell phenotype, and the potential complexity of receptor signaling outcomes in different cellular contexts.

Recommendations to Enhance Scientific Rigor:

2. Experimental Validation Strategy:
   - Suggest in vitro (e.g., using colonic epithelial cell lines) and in vivo (e.g., mouse models with controlled diets and receptor knockouts) experiments to test each step of the proposed pathway.

3. Address Receptor Context-Dependence:
   - Clarify whether the pro-oncogenic signaling via TGR5 and FXR is specific to colonic tissues in the setting of a high-fat diet, particularly given the potential for receptor functions to vary across tissues and conditions.

4. Integrate Multi-Omics Approaches:
   - Combine transcriptomic (gene expression), epigenomic, and metabolomic analyses to comprehensively map the changes from dietary intervention to molecular outcomes in epithelial cells.

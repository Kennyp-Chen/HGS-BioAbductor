 The hypothesis proposes designing a next-generation CAR-T cell targeting CLDN18.2 in gastric cancer. These CAR-T cells would be engineered with three key modifications:

  • Expression of the chemokine receptor CCR6 to facilitate improved trafficking to tumor sites.
 The overall goal is to create CAR-T cells that not only target tumor cells effectively but are also metabolically and immunologically primed to overcome tumor microenvironment challenges, thereby enhancing tumor cell lysis and overall therapeutic outcomes.

 The integration of metabolic reprogramming (by upregulating glycolytic enzymes) with immune checkpoint knockdown and enhanced trafficking represents an innovative multi-pronged approach. While individual aspects—such as targeting CLDN18.2 with CAR-T cells, CRISPR-mediated checkpoint inhibition, and modulation of cellular metabolism—have been explored in various studies, combining all three strategies into a single CAR-T cell design for gastric cancer is a novel synthesis in the field. This integrative strategy could distinguish the hypothesis from more conventional CAR-T designs, showing promise for enhanced efficacy in a challenging tumor microenvironment.

  – Knocking down inhibitory checkpoints (PD-1, Tim-3) is supported by evidence that these molecules limit T cell activation and persistence, suggesting that their inhibition might prolong CAR-T functionality.
  – Integrating CCR6 to improve trafficking leverages the role of chemokine receptors in guiding immune cells to the tumor, a concept that has merit in the context of tumors expressing the corresponding chemokine ligands.
 Evidence and Theoretical Grounding:
  – Each individual modification has a basis in current immuno-oncology research; however, the combined effect of these alterations in a clinically relevant model needs robust preclinical validation.

  – The complete knockdown of checkpoints like PD-1 and Tim-3 may risk uncontrolled T cell activation, potentially leading to off-target effects or autoimmunity.
  – While CCR6 expression is proposed to improve homing, its functional efficacy depends on the presence and gradient of its ligand in the tumor tissue; this relationship may vary between patients.
 Overall, the hypothesis is logically coherent and ambitious, but it hinges on the assumption that the combined modifications will act synergistically without introducing new complications.

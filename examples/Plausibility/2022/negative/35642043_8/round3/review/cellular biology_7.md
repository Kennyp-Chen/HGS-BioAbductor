1. Hypothesis Summary
──────────────────────────────
The hypothesis proposes that in the inflammatory microenvironment of many solid tumors, elevated cytokine levels—particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α)—activate post‐translational modification (PTM) enzymes, including specific glycosyltransferases and kinases. These enzymes are thought to drive targeted glycosylation and phosphorylation changes on the tight junction protein CLDN18.2. The modifications may disrupt tight junction integrity and reconfigure the protein’s extracellular domain to expose novel binding epitopes. Using modern technological platforms—machine learning for proteomics, CRISPR-based functional screens, and high-resolution imaging—the hypothesis suggests that these modifications can be identified and subsequently pharmacologically manipulated. Enhancing or modulating CLDN18.2’s PTM status is proposed as a strategy to improve the efficacy of CLDN18.2-directed immunotherapies (such as monoclonal antibodies and CAR-T cells), ultimately leading to better anti-tumor responses and improved patient outcomes.

• Therapeutic Implications: The rationale that pharmacological modulation of CLDN18.2’s PTM status might enhance immunotherapy is promising, yet the hypothesis must account for tumor heterogeneity and potential compensatory signaling pathways within the microenvironment.

Overall, the chain of reasoning is explicit and logically sound, though it would benefit from additional mechanistic support and experimental evidence demonstrating the causal links between cytokine-driven PTMs, altered tight junction properties, and enhanced immunotherapeutic efficacy.

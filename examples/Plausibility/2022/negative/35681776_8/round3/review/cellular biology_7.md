1. Hypothesis Summary:
• The hypothesis proposes that hepatocyte-specific nanoparticles can deliver a dual-targeted CRISPR/Cas9 system to liver cancer (HCC) cells.
• This gene-editing approach is designed to concurrently suppress survivin (an inhibitor of apoptosis) and modulate key epigenetic regulators (DNMT1 and HDACs) to reprogram the tumor microenvironment.
• By repressing these targets, the strategy is expected to restore apoptotic processes and reduce exosomal non-coding RNAs that promote immune evasion.
• This reprogramming is anticipated to trigger immunogenic cell death, evidenced by calreticulin exposure, ATP, and HMGB1 release, which in turn should enhance dendritic cell activation and CD8+ T cell recruitment.
• When combined with PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade, the approach seeks to remove residual T cell inhibition, thereby synergistically enhancing anti-tumor immune responses.
• The use of hepatocyte-specific nanoparticle engineering is intended to improve targeting specificity and control over CRISPR delivery, aiming for increased efficacy and safety compared to existing therapeutic methods.

• Combining these gene-editing effects with immunogenic cell death induction and subsequent PD-1 checkpoint blockade creates a multi-layered strategy that distinguishes it from more conventional monotherapies.
• While individual elements – such as nanoparticle-mediated targeted delivery, CRISPR-mediated gene editing, and checkpoint blockade – are actively researched, their integrated use to simultaneously reshape tumor immunology in HCC is innovative. 
• Overall, the hypothesis offers a fresh perspective on combinatorial cancer therapies and positions itself at the intersection of gene editing, epigenetic modulation, and immunotherapy.

1. Observation extraction:
 • Abstract 1: Radiation of liver tumors upregulates ICAM-1 on tumor cells to promote NK cell engagement via LFA-1. This enhances NK cell–mediated cytolysis against both parental and stem-like liver cancers.
 • Abstract 2: Low‐dose radiation of the spleen in liver tumor–bearing animals reduces immunosuppressive regulatory T cell (Treg) markers (including CTLA-4) and function, correlating with improved immune responses and smaller tumors.
 • Abstract 3: Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) in liver cancer patients is associated with immunophenotypic shifts, including reductions in circulating CD3⁺ T cells and certain NK cell subsets, pointing to measurable alterations in the immune landscape after radiation.

2. Causal analysis (individual):
 • For Abstract 1:
  – Already established cause? Yes. It is well documented that radiotherapy induces stress responses that upregulate adhesion molecules (like ICAM-1), promoting NK cell binding and tumor killing.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Even if radiation also induced Artemin in NSCLC (as hypothesized), the enhanced NK cell targeting via ICAM-1 is a separate, well‐recognized mechanism. Thus, this observation is not uniquely explained by an Artemin-mediated process.
  – Explanation: This is not a missing piece because the NK cell–tumor interaction via LFA-1/ICAM-1 is already established.
 • For Abstract 2:
  – Already established cause? Yes. The modulation of Treg cell function via radiation (reflected in changes in CTLA-4 and cytokine signatures) is a known immunomodulatory effect.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: If the hypothesis were true in NSCLC, one would expect radiotherapy to upregulate Artemin to trigger an immunosuppressive circuit that enhances Treg function. Instead, this study shows that low-dose radiation reduces Treg suppressive capacity.
  – Explanation: This observation does not serve as a novel explanation for immunosuppression via an Artemin loop; in fact, it suggests an opposite or at least a different modulation of Tregs, hence “not a missing piece.”
 • For Abstract 3:
  – Already established cause? Partially. Radiation is known to affect lymphocyte counts and phenotype, often transiently depleting certain T cell populations.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: The hypothesis predicts that radiation-induced Artemin would suppress CD8⁺ T cell activation and infiltration. Although a reduction in circulating CD3⁺ T cells was observed, this change is generally attributed to radiation’s direct effects (and possibly sequestration into tissues), and not uniquely to an Artemin-driven mechanism.
  – Explanation: Therefore, this observation is also not uniquely explained by the hypothesized Artemin loop; better known radiation effects already provide an explanation.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: While radiation-induced changes in immune cell populations (e.g., altered T cell and NK cell profiles) are expected with any radiotherapy, the specific mechanism proposed by the hypothesis—rapid upregulation of Artemin leading to an immunosuppressive network—is not directly supported by these observations. Instead, established mechanisms (such as the LFA-1/ICAM-1 axis in NK cell recognition and the direct modulation of Treg function) seem more likely to account for the observed effects.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: The data from Abstract 2, where low-dose radiation reduces Treg cell inhibitory function (contrasting with an Artemin-driven increase in immunosuppression), pose a potential contradiction. Also, the robust activation of NK cell responses in Abstract 1 suggests that radiation is enhancing anti-tumor immunity in some contexts rather than predominantly promoting an immunosuppressive network. Although differences in tumor type (liver cancer vs. NSCLC) are noted, the available observations contradict the expectation that radiation universally induces an immunosuppressive loop via Artemin.

5. Conclusion:
 The observations from these liver cancer studies are largely explained by already established radiotherapy effects on adhesion molecule expression, Treg cell function, and immune cell population dynamics. They do not provide support for an additional, novel Artemin-driven immunosuppressive network as proposed in the hypothesis for NSCLC. Therefore, while the hypothesized mechanism could theoretically operate in NSCLC, the observations from the abstracts suggest that other explanations are more likely to account for the radiation-induced immunologic changes.

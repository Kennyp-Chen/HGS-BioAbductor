1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis posits that alcohol consumption leads to epigenetic deregulation of gut epithelial microRNAs. This deregulation disrupts the regulation of tight junction proteins, compromising gut barrier integrity and resulting in increased intestinal permeability. Consequently, endotoxins (or bacterial products) translocate from the gut lumen into systemic circulation, leading to liver inflammation and activation of hepatic stellate cells. Furthermore, this cascade is compounded by metabolic stress and underlying genetic predispositions, which together promote the progression towards fibrosis and hepatocarcinogenesis (liver cancer) in the context of alcohol-associated liver disease.

While alcohol’s role in compromising barrier function and promoting liver disease has been documented, the focus on gut microRNAs as critical mediators is relatively innovative. The addition of metabolic stress and genetic predisposition further broadens the scope and adds complexity. Thus, the hypothesis offers an innovative perspective by merging epigenetic regulation, microRNA biology, and gut-liver axis dysfunction into a unified pathogenic mechanism for alcohol-associated liver disease.

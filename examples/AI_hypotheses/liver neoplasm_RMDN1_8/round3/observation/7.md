1. Observation extraction:
 • Observation 1: In a subset of human hepatocellular carcinomas (HCCs), HDAC1 is overexpressed, and its inactivation leads to reduced tumor growth, cell cycle arrest, and induction of autophagic cell death.
 • Observation 2: HDAC6, typically endowed with tumor suppressor functions in liver cancer, is functionally inactivated via miR-221 induction driven by JNK/c-Jun and NF-κB signaling.
 • Observation 3: HDAC2 is upregulated in HCC and promotes malignant progression via a mTORC1/NF-κB-dependent mechanism that sustains its own expression and enhances oncogenic signaling.

2. Causal analysis (individual):
 Observation 1 (HDAC1 overexpression and oncogenic function):
  a. Its cause is already established in the literature as a driver of oncogenesis through mis-regulation of cell cycle and autophagy pathways.
  b. Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: If upregulation of RMDN1 redirected HDAC1 toward tumor suppressive activity by repressive chromatin remodeling, we would expect a different functional outcome from HDAC1 rather than its clear oncogenic role. In the observed cases, HDAC1 appears to promote tumor progression.
  c. Novel explanation? – Not a missing piece. The established data already explain HDAC1’s role, and the hypothesis would need to overcome this well‐documented oncogenic function with additional supporting evidence for RMDN1 involvement.

 Observation 2 (HDAC6 suppression by miR-221):
  a. Its causal pathway is established via microRNA regulation leading to loss of a tumor suppressor function.
  b. Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: The hypothesis focuses on RMDN1 modulating HDAC1 (and indirectly other epigenetic regulators) to enforce a tumor-suppressive repressive chromatin state. It does not address HDAC6 regulation, so HDAC6’s behavior remains unrelated to the proposed mechanism.
  c. Novel explanation? – Not a missing piece. This observation is explained by a separate, already established pathway.

 Observation 3 (HDAC2 upregulation and its oncogenic positive feedback loop):
  a. Its cause is already established via growth factor signaling (mTORC1/NF-κB) that drives HDAC2 expression and sustains oncogenic signaling.
  b. Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: The hypothesis does not encompass HDAC2’s role; it focuses solely on a potential context-dependent tumor suppressor function of HDAC1 mediated by RMDN1. Thus, HDAC2’s oncogenic behavior is independent and unaffected by the proposed RMDN1 mechanism.
  c. Novel explanation? – Not a missing piece, as this pathway has been well described.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: In principle, if RMDN1 upregulation could rewire the epigenetic network by interacting with HDAC1 and co-repressors, then in a specific subset of liver tumors one might expect a corresponding shift in chromatin accessibility and repression of oncogenes. However, none of the studies reported such an RMDN1-dependent switch; instead, they document HDACs (particularly HDAC1 and HDAC2) driving oncogenic programs and HDAC6 loss contributing to tumorigenesis. Thus, the hypothesis does not provide a novel explanation for any subset of the presented observations based on current evidence.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: The observation of HDAC1’s clear oncogenic role in HCC (Observation 1) contradicts the notion that HDAC1 can be repurposed toward tumor suppression by RMDN1. Additionally, there is no evidence provided of decreased chromatin accessibility or increased repressive marks in the described tumors—features the hypothesis expects to see if the context-dependent repressive complex were formed. Therefore, the existing observations tend to conflict with the hypothesis’s proposed mechanism.

5. Conclusion:
 The hypothesis proposes that upregulation of RMDN1 may reprogram HDAC1’s activity in a subset of liver cancers into a tumor suppressor role by establishing a repressive epigenetic network. However, current observations predominantly support the established oncogenic activities of HDAC1, HDAC2, and the independent role of HDAC6 suppression via miR-221. There is no demonstrated evidence for an RMDN1-mediated switch in HDAC1 function or the associated chromatin changes in the provided abstracts.

1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis proposes that RMDN1 functions as a context‐dependent regulatory scaffold in liver neoplasms. Specifically, when RMDN1 is upregulated, it facilitates the assembly of chromatin remodeling complexes by recruiting histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby inducing chromatin compaction, suppressing oncogenic transcription, and decelerating tumor progression. Conversely, when RMDN1 is downregulated, the failure to recruit these repressive chromatin modifiers permits chromatin activators to bind more readily, resulting in the activation of pro-metastatic and angiogenic pathways. The hypothesis also posits that RMDN1’s modulatory function is modulated by its interactions with additional chromatin modifiers such as KDM3A and NSD1. The authors intend to test this dual-function model using advanced techniques, including CRISPR-driven epigenetic modifications, single-cell ATAC-seq for probing chromatin accessibility, and quantitative proteomics to map alterations in the RMDN1 interactome.

2. Originality Assessment

The hypothesis is original in its attempt to frame RMDN1 as a bifunctional regulator in liver neoplasms—a role that, if validated, would extend our current understanding of chromatin remodeling in cancer biology. While the involvement of HDACs and other chromatin modifiers (like KDM3A and NSD1) in cancer is well-established, positioning RMDN1 as a context-dependent scaffold that either suppresses or promotes tumor progression depending on its expression level provides a novel and innovative perspective. This dualistic model challenges the conventional one-directional view of oncogenic processes by highlighting a regulatory molecule that can play opposing roles in tumor biology.

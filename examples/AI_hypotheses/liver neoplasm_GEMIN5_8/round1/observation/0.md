1. Observation extraction:
 • In HCC, GOLM1 is overexpressed and its high expression is linked to enhanced angiogenesis (through activation of the VEGF signaling pathway) and altered immune cell infiltration.
 • In breast cancer (and partly in liver cancer models), GNG5 overexpression is associated with enhanced proliferation, migration, invasion, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metabolic changes, while its knockdown reduces these processes.
 • In HCC, GCN5 is overexpressed where its downregulation impairs cell proliferation and tumor formation, acting via downstream targets (such as AIB1) that modulate cell cycle and growth.

2. Causal analysis (individual observations):

Observation 1 (GOLM1 overexpression and angiogenesis in HCC):
 a. The cause for enhanced angiogenesis in these HCC cases is already established via VEGF-related signaling and known network interactions.
 b. Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: If GEMIN5 were upregulated and impaired double-strand break repair leading to genomic instability, it is plausible that resulting mutation accumulation could contribute in part to angiogenic behavior. However, the established mechanism (GOLM1 activating VEGF signaling) provides a more direct and supported explanation.
 c. Explanation: This observation is not a “missing piece” for the GEMIN5 hypothesis as the link to VEGF activation and angiogenesis is already well‐characterized.

Observation 2 (GNG5’s role in proliferation, EMT, and metabolic changes in BC and cancer models):
 a. The causal factors behind these processes are already partially explained by altered signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin) and metabolic reprogramming induced by GNG5.
 b. Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: In principle, genomic instability caused by impaired DNA repair (if GEMIN5 were involved) could contribute to oncogenic transformation and metastasis. Yet, the immediate trigger of EMT and metabolic alteration observed here is better attributed to GNG5’s signaling activity.
 c. Explanation: This does not represent a novel explanation for these observed phenomena; hence, it is “not a missing piece.”

Observation 3 (GCN5 overexpression promoting HCC progression via AIB1):
 a. The proliferative advantage in HCC via GCN5 is already explained by its role in transcriptional regulation (via AIB1 and cell cycle modulation).
 b. Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: While impaired DNA repair can fuel genomic instability and indirectly support tumor progression, the direct mechanistic link between GCN5-driven AIB1 expression and tumor growth provides a more immediate, established cause.
 c. Explanation: This observation, too, is “not a missing piece” for the GEMIN5 hypothesis.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: While genomic instability from impaired double-strand break repair (as posited for GEMIN5 upregulation) can eventually facilitate processes such as metastasis and angiogenesis, the observed phenomena are already robustly explained by alternate, well-defined mechanisms (e.g., GOLM1’s activation of VEGF signaling; GNG5’s role in EMT and metabolic reprogramming; GCN5’s regulation of cell cycle proteins). Thus, the GEMIN5 hypothesis does not currently offer a novel or necessary causal explanation for these observations.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: None of the reported observations directly contradict the idea that genomic instability can enhance tumor progression. However, each observation already has an established explanation that does not invoke GEMIN5-mediated impairment of double-strand break repair. This diminishes the necessity or the uniqueness of the GEMIN5 hypothesis in accounting for these findings.

5. Conclusion:
 The observations indicate that the tumor-promoting features (angiogenesis, proliferation, EMT) in liver and breast cancers are already explained by the overexpression and activity of genes like GOLM1, GNG5, and GCN5 with well‐established causal pathways. While the GEMIN5 hypothesis could in theory contribute to genomic instability and hence to tumor progression, it does not fill a gap unaddressed by the established mechanisms.

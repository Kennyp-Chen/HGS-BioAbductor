1. Hypothesis Summary:
 The hypothesis posits that in liver neoplasms, the deleterious prognostic impact of high MCM5 expression is not solely due to its independent effect but results from its interplay with key DNA repair regulators, specifically RAD51 and ATR. It proposes that MCM5 upregulation increases replication stress in tumor cells, triggering break-induced replication—a compensatory repair mechanism. However, if RAD51 and ATR are either underactive or abnormally regulated, the repair process becomes error-prone, leading to genomic instability. This instability, in turn, drives aggressive tumor phenotypes such as enhanced metastasis and angiogenesis, culminating in poorer clinical outcomes. The hypothesis further suggests that targeting this regulatory network could provide a novel therapeutic strategy.

It links the sensitization of tumor cells to replication stress with a compensatory but error-prone repair process, offering a fresh angle on how genomic instability might be mechanistically connected to clinical outcomes in liver cancer. Thus, the hypothesis adds value by combining multiple known fields in a unique context and could distinguish itself from existing studies if supported by robust experimental data.

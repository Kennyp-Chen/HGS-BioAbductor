1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis posits that in liver neoplasms, overexpression of the MCM5 protein increases replication stress. This stress shifts the cellular DNA repair machinery from high-fidelity repair to an error-prone double-strand break repair mechanism, specifically via break-induced replication (BIR). It further suggests that MCM5 acts in concert with other DNA repair proteins—namely RAD52 and potentially BRCA1—to modulate enzymatic activities involved in strand invasion and DNA polymerization during the repair process. As a consequence, this aberrant pathway contributes to genomic instability, thereby elevating the risk of metastasis and leading to poorer clinical outcomes. Conversely, the hypothesis implies that downregulation of these key genes could alleviate the dysfunctional repair process and potentially slow oncogenic progression.

2. Originality Assessment

The hypothesis introduces an integrative view by linking MCM5 upregulation with a switch in DNA repair mechanisms specifically through break-induced replication, implicating the cooperative action of RAD52 and BRCA1. While the roles of replication stress and error-prone repair in cancer progression are established in the literature, the focused application of these concepts to liver neoplasms via MCM5 is relatively novel. The emphasis on a cooperative interaction among multiple repair proteins (MCM5, RAD52, and possibly BRCA1) to drive a specific repair pathway that enhances genomic instability is innovative, particularly under the hypothesis that modulation of these genes’ expression levels can directly influence metastatic potential and clinical outcomes. However, the hypothesis straddles established knowledge and emerging concepts, meaning that while parts of it derive from known mechanisms, the particular constellation of factors and their proposed interactions in liver cancer represent a promising and distinctive perspective in clinical oncology research.

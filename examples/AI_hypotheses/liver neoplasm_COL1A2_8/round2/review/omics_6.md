1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis posits that in liver neoplasms, the overexpression of COL1A2 leads to excessive collagen deposition and remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM), increasing its stiffness. This altered microenvironment then activates integrin signaling and its downstream effectors (FAK, PI3K, ROCK), which in turn transmit mechanical and biochemical signals into the nucleus. These signals purportedly disrupt the nuclear pore complex (NPC) by downregulating or impairing key mRNA export factors (e.g., NUP62 and NXF1). The resulting defect in mRNA export is suggested to disturb the expression balance of genes regulating cell cycle and apoptosis, thereby fostering persistent activation of pathways that promote metastasis and angiogenesis—ultimately contributing to a more aggressive tumor phenotype and worse clinical outcomes.

2. Originality Assessment

The hypothesis integrates multiple well-recognized concepts in tumor biology, such as ECM remodeling, mechanotransduction, and altered mRNA transport; however, several aspects stand out:
• The specific linkage of COL1A2-induced ECM stiffness to the regulation of mRNA export factors via mechanotransduction is innovative. While both ECM stiffness effects and NPC alterations have been studied independently, explicitly connecting the overexpression of COL1A2 to a dysregulated mRNA export process via integrin/FAK/PI3K/ROCK signaling offers a novel mechanistic perspective.
• The inclusion of nuclear pore components (such as NUP62 and NXF1) as mechanistic endpoints is a creative angle, potentially opening new avenues for understanding liver cancer aggressiveness.
Overall, the hypothesis distinguishes itself by combining these pathways in a sequential cascade, making it a compelling, forward-thinking integration that stands apart from more traditional views of ECM’s role in cancer biology.

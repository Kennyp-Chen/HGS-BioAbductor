1. Observation extraction:
 • Abstract 1: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression is promoted by a circRNA (circ_0008194) that sponges miR-190a to relieve inhibition on AHNAK. This elevates AHNAK expression and, through the promotion of EMT, facilitates invasion and metastasis.
 • Abstract 2: NEK2 overexpression in HCC enhances tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis via activation of PI3K/AKT/NF‐κB signaling, MMP activation, and even mediates drug resistance.
 • Abstract 3: HDAC2 is upregulated in HCC through mTORC1/NF-κB signaling. This upregulation supports a positive feedback loop via AKT phosphorylation and influences EMT markers (Snail induction/E-cadherin suppression), thereby contributing to malignant progression.

2. Causal analysis (individual):
 • For Abstract 1 – circRNA/AHNAK axis:
  – The cause (circRNA sponging to upregulate AHNAK) is already experimentally established.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Although the hypothesis involves AHNAK2 instead of AHNAK, if AHNAK2 were to modulate protein geranylgeranylation to promote metastasis, it might also lead to enhanced metastasis. However, the established mechanism here (miRNA regulation by a circRNA) already explains the metastatic drive. Thus, the AHNAK2–geranylgeranylation pathway is neither required nor clearly indicated by these data; it is not a missing piece.
 • For Abstract 2 – NEK2-mediated oncogenesis:
  – The cause behind NEK2’s effect on metastasis, angiogenesis, and drug resistance is already detailed (through PI3K/AKT/NF‐κB and MMP activation).
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Metastatic and angiogenic features are seen in both the NEK2 study and the hypothesis. However, the causal factor in NEK2’s case is NEK2’s overexpression and its downstream effectors—not protein geranylgeranylation mediated by AHNAK2. Therefore, the hypothesis does not offer a novel or required explanation for these observations; it is not a missing piece.
 • For Abstract 3 – HDAC2-driven progression:
  – The mechanism behind HDAC2 upregulation is clearly established as involving growth factor-induced mTORC1/NF-κB signaling, and the downstream effects on AKT phosphorylation and EMT markers explain the aggressive behaviors.
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: While the aggressive cancer phenotypes such as increased metastasis could be shared, the specific mechanistic details in this observation are fully explained by growth factor/mTORC1/NF-κB signaling and not via changes in protein geranylgeranylation. Thus, the hypothesis does not provide a novel causal insight here either; it is not a missing piece.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: In general, if AHNAK2 overexpression modulated protein geranylgeranylation and thereby promoted metastasis and angiogenesis, one might expect a similar aggressive phenotype as observed. However, the observations in these abstracts have their own established pathways (circRNA sponging of miR-190a to upregulate AHNAK, NEK2-driven signaling, and HDAC2 activation via mTORC1/NF-κB) that already account for the oncogenic processes. While the outcome—enhanced metastasis and angiogenesis—is compatible with the hypothesis, the mechanistic explanations provided in these studies do not leave an explanatory gap that would necessitate invoking AHNAK2-mediated protein geranylgeranylation.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: None of the observations directly contradict the possibility that AHNAK2 overexpression might contribute to cancer progression; however, each observation already has a well‐characterized causal mechanism. This suggests that the established pathways are sufficient and that the addition of AHNAK2 modulating geranylgeranylation is neither required nor uniquely supported by these data.

5. Conclusion:
 The hypothesis that AHNAK2 overexpression contributes to liver neoplasm progression via modulation of protein geranylgeranylation—and its collaboration with RAB family genes—could conceivably produce metastatic and angiogenic phenotypes. However, the three abstracts provide established mechanisms (circRNA-AHNAK sponging, NEK2 signaling, and mTORC1/NF-κB–mediated HDAC2 activation) that already explain these aggressive cancer features without invoking this novel pathway. Consequently, while the hypothesis is not disproved by these observations, other explanations are more likely.

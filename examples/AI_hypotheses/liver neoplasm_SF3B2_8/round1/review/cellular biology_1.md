1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis claims that high expression of the splicing factor SF3B2 drives liver neoplasm progression by enhancing the integration of viral DNA into the host genome. The central idea is that SF3B2 overexpression causes mis-splicing or deregulation of key genes involved in genomic stability—specifically RAD51 and TP53—which undermines normal DNA repair mechanisms. This impaired repair promotes an intracellular environment conducive to viral DNA integration. Once integrated, viral elements may trigger oncogenic changes by altering chromatin structure, initiating pro-metastatic signaling, and promoting increased angiogenesis. The hypothesis further postulates that SF3B2 interacts with these regulatory genes in a positive feedback loop that accelerates tumor progression, thus establishing a potential causal link between RNA splicing dysregulation and aggressive liver cancer behavior.

2. Originality Assessment

While the role of splicing factors in cancer has been increasingly recognized, this hypothesis offers a relatively novel angle by connecting SF3B2 overexpression not only to aberrant RNA splicing but also to a facilitative role in viral DNA integration—a process more conventionally associated with viral oncogenesis. The connection between mis-splicing of genes like RAD51 and TP53 (both crucial for genomic integrity) and subsequent enhancement of viral integration is not a mainstream or widely explored concept in liver cancer research. Therefore, this proposal is innovative insofar as it combines RNA splicing abnormalities with viral integration as dual contributors to oncogenesis, offering a fresh perspective that merits further exploration.

1. Hypothesis Summary

The hypothesis posits that in liver neoplasms, upregulation of the splicing factor SF3B2 works in concert with key regulatory genes ATM and TP53 to alter both splicing machinery and DNA repair mechanisms. This altered state purportedly increases the host genome’s vulnerability to viral DNA integration by (a) stabilizing splicing complexes that favor integration events and (b) impairing genomic surveillance. The cascade of these events is proposed to trigger downstream oncogenic signals that promote metastasis and angiogenesis, ultimately contributing to poorer cancer outcomes. The hypothesis seeks to establish a mechanistic link connecting splicing regulation, viral integration, and cancer progression, while also proposing potential therapeutic targets.

──────────────────────────────
2. Originality Assessment

This hypothesis introduces an innovative perspective by linking SF3B2-mediated splicing alterations with the process of viral DNA integration in liver cancers—a connection that is not widely established in current literature. Although SF3B2, ATM, and TP53 are well-known individually for their roles in RNA splicing and genomic stability, proposing their combined effect in enhancing susceptibility to viral integration represents a novel conceptual framework. In essence, the hypothesis attempts to integrate several well-studied molecular mechanisms in an unorthodox manner, potentially opening up new therapeutic avenues if validated.

While their roles in genomic surveillance and repair are well documented, their direct interaction with splicing machinery—and more specifically with SF3B2—in the context of viral integration is not commonly reported, and the hypothesis would benefit from a clearer explanation of these interactions.

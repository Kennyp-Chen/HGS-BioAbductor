1. Observation extraction:
 • Abstract 1 shows that SF3B1 is overexpressed in HCC, correlating with tumor aggressiveness, changes in oncogenic splice variants (e.g., KLF6-SV1, BCL-XL), and altered signaling. Inhibition of SF3B1 reduces proliferation, migration, and oncogenic features in vitro and in vivo.
 • Abstract 2 demonstrates that dysregulation of several splicing factors (e.g., SRSF3, SRSF6, PSF) occurs early in liver damage and HCC. Changes in SF expression modulate alternative splicing events (e.g., INSR isoforms) and are linked with tumor recurrence risk, though some increased SF expression even correlates with prolonged overall survival.
 • Abstract 3 reports that dysregulation of distinct spliceosomal elements (e.g., EIF4A3, RBM3) in HCC is associated with tumor aggressiveness and recurrence, with specific alterations in the splicing of genes like FGFR4 mediating oncogenic effects.

2. Causal analysis (individual):
 a. Observation from Abstract 1:
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: If SF3B2 upregulation were enhancing viral DNA integration through modulation of alternative splicing, one might expect a specific signature linked to viral integration events. However, the observation focuses on SF3B1 and the generation of oncogenic splice variants without mention of viral DNA integration.
  – Cause already established? Yes – SF3B1 upregulation and its direct impact on splice variant expression have been documented independently.
  – Novel explanation? Not a missing piece. The data are better explained by SF3B1-mediated splicing alterations rather than the proposed role of SF3B2 in viral integration.
 b. Observation from Abstract 2:
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: Dysregulation of splicing factors in early liver damage is observed, but the study focuses on factors such as SRSF3 and SRSF6. The hypothesis posits an involvement of SF3B2 (and interplay with SRSF1) in viral integration, a mechanism not implicated by these findings.
  – Cause already established? The splicing changes in liver disease appear to be part of already recognized processes during liver pathogenesis.
  – Novel explanation? No; the hypothesis does not provide a superior or novel causal pathway to explain the observed SF dysregulation.
 c. Observation from Abstract 3:
  – Would we see this observation if the hypothesis was true: The alteration of spliceosomal components (e.g., EIF4A3) and their impact on splicing of key oncogenic genes (like FGFR4) are observed. This supports a model where splicing dysregulation drives tumor aggressiveness via altered gene isoforms, not via mechanisms involving viral DNA integration.
  – Cause already established? Yes – the modulation of splicing factors (other than SF3B2) clearly influences oncogenic signaling.
  – Novel explanation? Again, the hypothesis’s emphasis on viral integration as a consequence of SF3B2 upregulation is not supported by these observations and thus does not fill a missing piece.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: The hypothesis implies that SF3B2 upregulation—and its interplay with SRSF1—facilitates viral DNA integration leading to genomic instability and oncogenic pathway activation. However, all three abstracts implicate alternative mechanisms (e.g., overexpression of SF3B1, EIF4A3, and alterations of other SFs) in driving oncogenesis through direct effects on splicing regulation of key target genes. None of the studies report or suggest a role for SF3B2 or viral integration events. Therefore, the hypothesis does not offer a novel explanation for the subset of observations presented.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: While no observation explicitly contradicts the possibility that splicing factors could affect viral integration, the studies provide strong evidence for alternative, well‐established causes of splicing dysregulation in HCC that do not involve SF3B2 or viral DNA integration. The absence of any ties to viral integration in these findings weakens the proposed causal link, suggesting that other explanations for the splicing alterations and tumor progression are more plausible.

5. Conclusion:
 The observations consistently document the role of splicing factor dysregulation (involving SF3B1, SRSF3, EIF4A3, and others) in modulating oncogenic splicing and signaling in HCC. The hypothesis that elevated SF3B2 promotes viral DNA integration by modulating alternative splicing (including that of SRSF1) is not supported by the data provided, and existing literature offers better established mechanisms for the observed phenomena.

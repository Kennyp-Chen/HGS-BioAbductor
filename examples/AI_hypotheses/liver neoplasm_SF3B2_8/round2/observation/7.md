1. Observation extraction:
 • Abstract 1 shows that SF3B1 is overexpressed in HCC and its high expression correlates with tumor aggressiveness, the production of oncogenic splice variants (KLF6‐SV1, BCL‐XL), and poor overall survival. Both genetic silencing and pharmacological inhibition of SF3B1 decrease cancer cell viability and tumor growth.
 • Abstract 2 demonstrates that changes in the profile of splicing factors occur early in liver disease. In HCC, several SFs (including the tumor‐suppressor SRSF3) are upregulated in tumor samples versus non-tumor tissues—with certain alternative splicing events (e.g., of the INSR gene) correlating with recurrence risk and survival.
 • Abstract 3 identifies consistent dysregulation of diverse spliceosomal components in HCC (e.g., overexpression of EIF4A3), linking these alterations with changes in key oncogenic splicing events (such as FGFR4 splicing) that promote tumor aggressiveness and decreased patient survival.

3. Causal analysis (summary):
 Would we see some of the observations if the hypothesis was true: Conceivably, yes—a model in which SF3B2 (in collaboration with ATM and TP53) drives both aberrant splicing and impaired DNA repair might result in oncogenic splice variants and promote aggressive disease. However, the observed dysregulation in HCC has been predominantly linked to other splice factors (SF3B1, SRSF3, EIF4A3) via mechanisms that are already experimentally validated. Moreover, the additional claims regarding genomic fragile sites and viral DNA integration have not been addressed in the presented abstracts. Therefore, while the hypothesis touches on relevant pathways, it does not offer a novel explanation for a subset of these observations because the major causal roles are already explained without invoking SF3B2 or its interactions with DNA repair custodians.

4. Disproof analysis:
 Does some observations disprove the hypothesis: None of the observations directly contradict a potential role for splicing factor dysregulation in HCC. However, the specifics of the hypothesis—namely, the emphasis on SF3B2 (rather than the observed SF3B1, EIF4A3, etc.), the combination with ATM and TP53 to impair DNA repair, and the role of genomic fragile sites as viral integration hotspots—are not supported by the existing data. Thus, while the observations do not disprove the existence of a dual mechanism, they indicate that other explanations (already established roles for other SFs in HCC) are more strongly supported by the evidence.

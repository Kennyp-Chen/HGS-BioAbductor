1. Hypothesis Summary  
The proposed hypothesis suggests that in liver neoplasms—particularly those associated with hepatitis virus—the upregulation of the splicing factor SF3B2, in cooperation with SRSF1, alters the splicing patterns of host genes critical for maintaining genomic integrity and regulating viral DNA integration. Specifically, elevated SF3B2 is postulated to favor a splicing shift in integrase cofactors and DNA repair regulators, thereby fostering a cellular milieu that enhances viral DNA integration. This integration is thought to cause localized genomic instability, which in turn triggers oncogenic signaling pathways related to metastasis and angiogenesis. The hypothesis is supported by a plan to deploy modern experimental methods (CRISPR-Cas9 knockouts, RNA sequencing including long-read techniques, and single-cell viral integration assays) to precisely characterize these splicing changes and their downstream impacts.

2. Originality Assessment  
The hypothesis exhibits an innovative perspective by linking RNA splicing alterations directly to viral integration events in liver cancer. While dysregulation of splicing factors (including SF3B2 and SRSF1) has been implicated in various cancers, integrating this concept with the specific modulation of integrase cofactors and DNA repair regulators represents a creative and potentially novel mechanistic insight. This approach distinguishes itself from more conventional pathways of oncogenesis by suggesting a coordinated relationship between splicing regulation and viral integration—two areas that are often studied independently. However, while the perspective is fresh, it will be important to establish whether this combined mechanism has been sufficiently differentiated from existing models of splicing deregulation or from known effects of viral integration in hepatocellular carcinoma.

3. Validity and Strength Evaluation  
Biologically, the hypothesis is plausible given that both SF3B2 and SRSF1 play significant roles in spliceosome function and that viral integration has been shown to contribute to genomic instability. The assertion that altered splicing of integrase cofactors and DNA repair regulators can reshape the cellular environment to favor viral integration is an intriguing concept. However, the logical pathway from splicing factor upregulation to enhanced viral integration presents several challenges:
  
• The causal relationship is complex—it assumes that the changes in alternative splicing directly affect the function of factors mediating viral integration, yet the specific molecular targets (individual integrase cofactors and DNA repair regulators) are not detailed.  
• Although the use of CRISPR-Cas9, advanced RNA-seq techniques, and single-cell assays is a robust strategy for investigating splicing events, the hypothesis would benefit from a more detailed mechanistic explanation and preliminary evidence linking these splicing shifts specifically to the viral integration process.  
• The link between splicing changes and downstream oncogenic signaling (metastasis and angiogenesis) via localized genomic instability rests on multiple steps that each require validation. Without intermediate data demonstrating that these altered splicing events indeed result in functional deficits in DNA repair or specific changes in integrase cofactor activity, the hypothesis risks being overly correlative.

  - Weakness: The hypothesis does not specify which integrase cofactors and DNA repair regulators are predicted to be affected by the splicing shift.

  - Weakness: The hypothesis currently centers on SF3B2 and SRSF1 without addressing the potential involvement of other splicing factors or general splicing dysregulation in liver neoplasms.

Below is a step‐by‐step analysis of the hypothesis:

1. Hypothesis Summary:
 The hypothesis proposes that in a subset of advanced liver cancers, the enzyme PFKFB2 is upregulated and collaborates with established metabolic regulators (like the ZEB1-PFKM and MYC-PGK1 axes). This upregulation not only boosts glycolysis to meet the energy needs of tumor cells but also diverts surplus glycolytic intermediates into the mevalonate pathway. The shift is posited to lead to increased expression and activation of prenylation enzymes (HMGCR and GGTases), which in turn enhance the geranylgeranylation of key small GTPases. These posttranslational modifications are proposed to promote cellular behaviors associated with metastasis (cell migration, angiogenesis, and invasion), thereby linking a reprogrammed metabolic state with more aggressive tumor phenotypes. The hypothesis further outlines that this metabolic rerouting can be monitored and validated through methods such as CRISPR gene editing, metabolic fluxomics, and single-cell proteomics.

2. Originality Assessment:
 The proposed hypothesis is innovative in linking PFKFB2—a glycolysis-regulating enzyme—to the diversion of glycolytic intermediates into the mevalonate pathway, a route classically associated with lipid biosynthesis and protein prenylation. While the Warburg effect and metabolic reprogramming in cancer have been long studied, the precise interplay between PFKFB2 and mevalonate pathway activation in liver neoplasms is a relatively underexplored territory. The inclusion of known regulators (ZEB1-PFKM and MYC-PGK1) provides context and supports the idea that PFKFB2 might work in concert with established oncogenic pathways. Moreover, the comprehensive multi-omics approach (CRISPR, fluxomics, and single-cell proteomics) adds a novel methodological perspective to dissecting this complex metabolic network. Overall, the hypothesis stands out by merging well-established mechanisms with a new regulatory layer that connects glycolysis directly to aggressive metastatic behavior through the mevalonate pathway.

3. Validity and Strength Evaluation:
 The logic of the hypothesis is based on established cancer metabolic alterations. Enhanced glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and rerouting of metabolites to biosynthetic pathways are both well-documented in tumors. By suggesting that excess glycolytic intermediates are funneled toward the mevalonate pathway, the hypothesis relies on a mechanistic plausibility: increased substrate availability could drive up lipid biosynthesis and protein prenylation, processes known to modulate cell signaling and behavior.
 However, several critical steps require robust evidence:
  • It assumes a functional and quantitative shift in metabolite flux from glycolysis to the mevalonate pathway solely based on PFKFB2 upregulation. The biochemical mechanisms governing such redirection must be clearly demonstrated.
  • The hypothesis connects increased mevalonate pathway activity with upregulation of key prenylation enzymes, yet the causal links (e.g., transcriptional regulation, allosteric activation) need explicit support.
  • The role of enhanced geranylgeranylation in driving migration and angiogenesis is plausible but involves a chain of multiple downstream events; each step must be validated to confirm that these modifications directly enhance metastatic potential in liver neoplasms.
  • While the experimental plan involving CRISPR, metabolic fluxomics, and proteomics is robust, integrating these diverse datasets to provide a cohesive mechanistic narrative could be challenging.
 Overall, the relationships proposed are logically coherent but depend on several assumptions that must be experimentally verified.

4. Conclusion and Expert Recommendations:
 Notable Weaknesses and Gaps:
  • The hypothesis presumes that the upregulation of PFKFB2 necessarily results in metabolic flux redirection into the mevalonate pathway without detailing the regulatory checkpoints or feedback mechanisms that typically balance these metabolic routes.
  • The link between increased glycolytic intermediates and the activation/upregulation of prenylation enzymes (HMGCR and GGTases) could benefit from further clarification—specifically, whether the regulation is direct (e.g., via metabolite-sensitive transcription factors or signaling cascades) or an indirect adaptive response.
  • A step-by-step connection between increased prenylation of GTPases and the functional outcomes (cell migration, angiogenesis, metastasis) needs additional mechanistic details and supporting data.
 Recommendations to Enhance the Hypothesis:
  • Provide or design studies to quantitatively measure flux through the glycolytic and mevalonate pathways using isotope-labeled substrates. This would directly address whether increased PFKFB2 levels shift metabolic pathways as proposed.
  • Investigate whether alterations in PFKFB2 expression directly or indirectly affect the transcriptional or posttranslational regulation of HMGCR and GGTases. This could involve promoter analysis, reporter assays, or chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing.
  • Validate the causal relationship between increased prenylation activity and metastatic behavior using targeted inhibition (e.g., chemical inhibitors of geranylgeranyl transferases) in cell migration and angiogenesis assays.
  • Consider potentially confounding factors and alternative pathways that might also direct glycolytic intermediates into the mevalonate pathway or promote metastasis, ensuring that the hypothesis remains focused yet comprehensive.
 Overall, while the hypothesis offers a fresh perspective on the interconnection between metabolic reprogramming and tumor aggressiveness in liver cancer, addressing these gaps with targeted experimental designs will greatly enhance its scientific rigor and persuasive power.
